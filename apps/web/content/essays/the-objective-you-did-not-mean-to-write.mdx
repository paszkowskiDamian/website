Picking a depth model should have been arithmetic. Feed each one a photograph of a carved relief, get back a height map, score it, take the&nbsp;winner.

The scoring had a term I was rather pleased with. Reliefs that come out too steep can't be cast in plaster — the mould won't draw — so the score penalised excessive slope. Sensible, I thought. It keeps the pipeline honest about what it's for.

Then I went back to it a few weeks later and wrote down what I actually
wanted, in one sentence: *I need the true depth map from the image. Making it
manufacturable is a later step.*

Which quietly demolished the term I was pleased with, for a reason that cuts
deeper than the sentence suggests.

## What the penalty was actually doing

Suppose the object in the photograph genuinely has a seventy-degree wall. Some carved letter, a chiselled border, an undercut fold in a bronze figure. The correct height map has a seventy-degree wall. That's not a flaw in the reconstruction. That's the reconstruction being *right*.

My metric docked points for it.

So a model that recovered the wall accurately scored below a model that smoothed it into a gentle ramp. Not because it hallucinated, not because it was noisy — because it told the truth about a surface that happens to be inconvenient downstream.

<PullQuote quote="The metric wasn't measuring accuracy with a manufacturing caveat. It was measuring accuracy minus accuracy, in the regions that mattered most." />

Worse, it was self-reinforcing. Every method that leans on a smoothness prior — and most of them do — got a free boost from the penalty on exactly the cases where its prior is wrong. I was selecting for the failure mode.

## Two stages that must not touch

The fix is boring to state and load-bearing in every direction.

**Stage one: reconstruction.** Recover the true height field of the surface in the picture. Fidelity is the only objective. If the real thing has a seventy-degree wall, the right output has a seventy-degree wall. No other consideration gets a vote.

**Stage two: adaptation.** A separate, downstream pass that takes an accurate map and makes it castable — clamping slope to the local draft budget, reshaping walls until the mould will draw. This already exists. It costs about 5% of the fine detail, which is a price worth naming out loud precisely *because* it's a price and not a free lunch.

Keeping them apart isn't tidiness. It's the difference between "we chose to give up 5% of the detail to make this castable" and "our depth model is mysteriously bad at steep walls and we're not sure why."

## The part I didn't expect

Here's what actually changed when the manufacturing term came out of the objective, and it's the reason this is worth writing down.

While castability was in there, the score could only ever be a **no-reference proxy**. There's no ground truth for "how castable is this, weighted against how accurate it is" — the weights are a taste judgement. So I was stuck scoring reconstructions by heuristics: does the histogram look right, is there too much flat area, do the slopes seem plausible. All of it circular. All of it arguing with itself.

Take manufacturability out, and the remaining objective is *accuracy*. Accuracy has ground truth. You render a known height field, run the render through the pipeline, and measure the error against the field you started from. No proxies, no weights, no taste.

<PullQuote quote="A clean objective isn't just more correct. It's the thing that makes the problem measurable at all." />

That reframing turned a months-long argument about which output looked better into an afternoon of arithmetic. It also immediately paid: measured against rendered truth, the ranking of methods was not the ranking my proxy scores had produced. The chain that won on fine detail — normals from one model, form from a different model's depth prior, fused — had been sitting mid-table under the old score.

There's a further sting. With real numbers available, the largest error turned out not to be fine detail at all. Across every method, the dominant term was **form**: an invented bow or tilt across the whole panel, a broad low-frequency warp that no amount of texture recovery touches. I had been optimising the ornament while the plaque itself was banana-shaped. The proxy score had no way to tell me that, because a smooth wrong surface looks exactly as plausible as a smooth right one.

## Where these get in

I don't think I wrote a stupid metric. I think I wrote the metric the way most people would, because the constraint was real and it was *right there*.

That's the pattern. The contaminating term always arrives as an obviously reasonable requirement from an adjacent stage. Latency budgets in a relevance score. Cost limits in a quality eval. Safety in a helpfulness rating. Every one of them is a genuine constraint on the system. None of them belong in the number that measures whether the model got the answer right.

The tell is worth memorising: **if satisfying your metric can require the output to be wrong about the world, the metric has stopped being a metric.** It's now a blend of a measurement and a preference, and once blended you can never again say which one moved.

## Keeping it clean

Two practices came out of this that I'd keep anywhere.

The measurement code still *reports* the manufacturing numbers — slope percentiles, a letter grade for castability, the lot. They print alongside the accuracy score. They are simply not inside it. You get to see both and decide; the ranking doesn't get to quietly absorb the trade-off on your behalf.

And when someone says "that setting produces something we can't manufacture," the correct response is not to reject the setting. It's to say what it costs in *accuracy* — often nothing — and hand the manufacturing problem to the stage that owns it.

The map should be true. Making it castable is a separate job, done later, by code that knows it's making a compromise.
