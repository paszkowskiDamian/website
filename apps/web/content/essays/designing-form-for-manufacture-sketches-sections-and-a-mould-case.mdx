## TL;DR

- FORM designs a cup and exports 3D-printable parts for making its plaster mould.
- Claude wrote the geometry quickly. Most of the time went into agreeing on what the parts should look like.
- Written descriptions led to wrong parts. Screenshots, marked-up renders and section drawings fixed them.
- A relief that works on a flat panel can lock the mould once it is wrapped around a cup, so FORM now adjusts the relief and places the mould seams.

FORM has two workbenches. In one you edit a relief as a height map. In the other you shape a cup from its side profile and wrap the relief around it. At the end, the design has to become a plaster mould that comes apart without damaging the&nbsp;cast.

![The form workbench: the side profile of a mug on the left and the 3D cup on the right](/form-profile-workbench.jpg "Fig. 01 · The form workbench. The side profile of a mug, with its foot and the recess underneath, is spun into the 3D preview. The seams and the mould are worked out from this profile.")

Slip-cast cups are made in plaster moulds. You make a master in the shape of the cup, pour plaster around it to make the mould, then pour liquid clay into the mould. Many workshops cast a silicone copy of the master first. I wanted to skip that step and pour plaster straight into 3D printed&nbsp;parts.

## The first attempt

I asked Claude to research plaster moulds for cups and write a generator. The first version put each plaster piece in a closed printed tray. The shapes were valid, but set plaster cannot be taken out of a closed tray. Claude rebuilt it with walls that come apart, and I sent screenshots of the mould kit I had already modelled in&nbsp;Rhino.

![Two Rhino screenshots: mould walls, half-cup masters and a split plug collar; and the front of the mould box](/form-rhino-mould-kit.jpg "Fig. 02 · Screenshots of my Rhino models, sent as reference. Left: box walls, half-cup masters on plates and a split plug collar. Right: the box from the front, with slanted walls to use less plaster.")

That round found real problems. The plug seat was too shallow, the wall joints would leak, and the parts only touched each other instead of being joined into solid pieces. We moved the geometry to a solid modelling library called&nbsp;`manifold-3d`.

## Explaining my Rhino method

A few days later I told Claude it had the approach wrong and walked it through how I build the mould in Rhino. Take the cup's side profile, add the foot and a 5 mm extension above the rim, spin half of it around the cup's axis, and stand that half on a flat 5 mm board. That half cup on its board is the master, and plaster poured around it becomes one half of the&nbsp;mould.

Claude repeated the method back and asked three questions before changing any code. From then on, the printed parts were the master and the box around it, and the tray generator was&nbsp;deleted.

## Where words went wrong

Small fixes went one screenshot at a time: a slope angle, lumpy shading, a top surface that should follow the underside of the cup. Longer written descriptions caused bigger mistakes. I once asked for a "triangular slot", meaning a notch in the edge of the board. Claude built a triangle sticking out of the pour opening. We caught it because the part was on&nbsp;screen.

The top plate of the box was the hardest part. Three rounds of written back-and-forth did not fix it, so I asked Claude to draw how it understood the plate. It drew a cross-section from the actual 3D model, with each piece labelled. I scribbled over the wrong area, Claude redrew it, and I marked up the second&nbsp;version.

![Two cross-section drawings of the top plate: Claude's first drawing with my scribble, and its redraw with my marks](/form-section-diagram-markup.jpg "Fig. 03 · Left: Claude's cross-section of the top plate as built, with my scribble over the wrong area. Right: Claude's redrawn version, with my marks on it.")

The second drawing settled it. I told Claude that I work better with diagrams, and it saved that for the project. After that it asked design questions as drawings: whether the dovetail rails should be 2 mm or 3 mm deep, how the end walls should clamp, and whether to close the top of the box. For the last one it pointed out that a closed top would seal the pour opening, so we left it&nbsp;open.

Marking up renders worked the other way&nbsp;round.

![Two renders I marked up: arrows at uneven plaster thickness, and a crossed-out wedge with the thin wall outlined](/form-plaster-cover-markup.jpg "Fig. 04 · Two renders I marked up. Left: arrows where the plaster was thicker than elsewhere. Right: the wedge crossed out and the thin wall I wanted outlined.")

The arrows became one rule: 20 mm of plaster all round the cup. The outline became a plain wall that stands upright for half its height and then leans in at&nbsp;45°.

## Getting the relief out of the mould

The relief on the cup has its own problem. On a flat panel, a raised pattern always comes out of a mould pulled straight up. Around a cup, the two halves of the mould pull sideways. Near the seams where the halves meet, the cup wall runs almost parallel to the pull, and even a gentle bump there can hook the&nbsp;plaster.

<PullQuote quote="Our first check tested reliefs lying flat. It passed all 49 test reliefs, and all 49 locked once they were wrapped around a cup." />

We fixed this in two places. The depth pipeline got a draft-limit step that softens only the slopes that would lock and keeps 95% of the fine detail. In the Studio, an easing step lowers peaks and fills hollows until every face can release. The mould seams are placed through the handle, because a handle cast with the cup only comes out if the mould splits through it. A casting assistant then suggests the fewest mould pieces that&nbsp;release.

![A relief band before and after the draft limit, rendered from four angles](/form-draft-limit-before-after.jpg "Fig. 05 · A relief band before (top) and after (bottom) the draft limit. The area that would lock went from 1.75% to 0%, and the pattern still reads clearly.")

![The unwrapped surface stage, with seams set by the handle and the casting assistant suggesting two body pieces and a base](/form-casting-assistant.jpg "Fig. 06 · The unwrapped surface stage. The seams are set by the handle, and the casting assistant suggests two body pieces and a base.")

## What gets printed

For the default mug, FORM lays out eight printed parts: two half masters on their boards, four box walls that slide and clamp together, and two shells for casting the plaster plug that fills the recess under the foot. The master is scaled up 13.6% to allow for the clay shrinking as it dries and is&nbsp;fired.

![The parts coming together in the assembly view: a half master on its board, the box walls and the plug mould](/form-master-assembly.jpg "Fig. 07 · The parts coming together: a half master on its board, the box walls and the plug mould.")

Two pieces are not built yet. The handle needs its own two-part mould, and the base needs its own master. The Studio shows both as&nbsp;warnings.

## Where the time went

Claude wrote the first mould generator in a day and replaced it within the week. Most of the time went into agreeing on what each part should look like, and screenshots and drawings got us there faster than written&nbsp;descriptions.
