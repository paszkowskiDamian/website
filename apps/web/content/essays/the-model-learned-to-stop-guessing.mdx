Off-the-shelf depth models are trained on rooms, streets and faces. Point one at a bronze plaque and it does something reasonable and useless: it tells you the plaque is a flat rectangle a metre away. The two millimetres of ornament on its face — the only part I care about — sit inside the rounding&nbsp;error.

So the model has to be taught. And to teach it, you need pictures whose true surface you already know, which real photographs conspicuously do not come with.

## Making the training data

You render it. Build a height field, light it, photograph it in software, and keep the geometry as the label. I've written before about [what it takes to make those renders look real](/essays/what-actually-closed-the-gap/); this is about what happened when a model actually ate them.

The corpus is Blender, headless, on the laptop GPU: a few thousand plaques at 1024 px, each with a matching label. Two decisions in there took a rebuild each to arrive at.

**The label is normals, not depth.** This is counterintuitive, because depth is what I want at the end. But an image is evidence of *slope* — shading is a function of the surface normal — and a normal map is therefore the honest thing to ask a model to predict from one picture. Depth is an integration away, and asking for it directly means asking the model to invent the constant of integration.

**The label comes from geometry, never from the renderer.** Cycles will happily give you a Normal pass. It is filtered and antialiased, which is exactly right for a picture and exactly wrong for a label: you'd be training on a slightly blurred version of the truth and calling the blur ground truth.

<PullQuote quote="Ask the model for the thing the image is evidence of. Everything else is a guess you're grading as if it were an observation." />

![Four synthetic relief renders above their matching normal-map labels](/synthetic-relief-render-and-label.jpg "Fig. 01 — Renders above, labels below. The flat, evenly-lit look in three of these is the aiming bug, caught after two thousand images.")

Then there was the composition problem. My first corpus was random domes and hatched grooves — technically height fields, statistically nothing like a plaque. Real relief has a vocabulary: a silhouette stepping up through a narrow rim, soft modelling inside it, seam grooves, hammered ground, rows of lettering. Train on noise that satisfies your constraints and you get a model that is excellent at noise.

## The bug that cost a corpus

Two thousand renders in, the images were dull. Not broken — dull. Flat, evenly lit, like an overcast day.

The key lights were aimed away from the panels. Blender's `to_track_quat` wants the vector pointing *toward* the thing you're aiming at, and I had handed it the light's own position. Every light in the scene was pointed out into the void, and the panels were lit entirely by the environment.

The part worth keeping: **the labels were perfectly fine.** Geometry doesn't care where the lights point. So nothing crashed, nothing was obviously corrupt, and the only symptom was an aesthetic impression I could have easily rationalised away as "renders look a bit CG." A whole corpus, quietly worth much less than it cost.

## The result, including the bit that looks like failure

The fine-tune itself is almost anticlimactic: a LoRA adapter on a small vision transformer, 518 px, five epochs, a couple of thousand samples, forty-eight minutes on a laptop.

On held-out synthetic renders, fine detail correlation went from **0.20 to 0.48**, and broad form from **0.57 to 0.71**. Encouraging, and also nearly meaningless — same renderer, same distribution, of course it improved.

The real test was a set of scanned museum reliefs: real photographs, real geometry, an entirely different imaging path. There, broad form went from **0.72 to 0.82** and fine detail from **0.34 to 0.38**. Smaller, and honest.

And one number went **down**: the share of fine detail energy the model recovered.

## Why the drop is the good news

My first read was that I'd traded detail for form. The truer read is more interesting.

The base model was scoring well on "detail recovered" by putting plausible texture more or less everywhere. Reliefs have texture; a model that hallucinates texture uniformly will correlate with *having* texture, and a metric that measures how much fine energy is present cannot tell invented detail from recovered detail. It rewards confident noise.

Fine-tuning on data where the fine band is real taught the model where detail genuinely is — and, necessarily, where it isn't. It stopped decorating the flat ground. Detail *correlation* went up, because the detail it does emit is now in the right places. Detail *energy* went down, because it emits less of it, having quit the habit of inventing some everywhere.

<PullQuote quote="A metric that counts how much detail is present cannot distinguish recovering it from making it up." />

That's the part I'd carry into any other model I train. When something improves, go and find which of your numbers got worse, and ask whether that number was measuring a virtue or a vice. Sometimes a score falls because the model got worse. Sometimes it falls because the score was rewarding a bad habit, and the model has just been talked out of it.

You cannot tell those apart from the leaderboard. You have to know what the metric is made of.
