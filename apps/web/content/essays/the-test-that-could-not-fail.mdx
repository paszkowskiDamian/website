My validator had never once failed anything, and for about three weeks I read that as good&nbsp;news.

The job was simple enough to describe. I generate reliefs — grayscale height maps that get wrapped around a ceramic mug and cast in plaster. Plaster moulds have to be pulled apart, which means the geometry can't have undercuts: no part of the surface can hook back under another part along the direction the mould is drawn. So I wrote a screen. It walked the height map, computed slopes, and reported whether anything was too steep to draw.

Forty-nine reliefs went through it. Forty-nine passed. Some of them were aggressive — deep grooves, near-vertical walls, the kind of thing that makes a potter wince. Still passed.

The mistake wasn't in the code. The code did exactly what it said. The mistake was that the question it answered had only one possible answer.

## A height map cannot undercut itself

A height map is a function: one height for every point on a plane. `z = f(x, y)`. That's the entire data structure.

Now ask whether such a surface can undercut a straight pull along `+Z`. It cannot. Not "usually doesn't" — cannot, ever, for any input. An undercut requires two surface points stacked along the pull direction with material hooking between them, and a function that returns a single `z` per `(x, y)` has no way to express that. It's a property of the representation, not of the artwork.

So my screen was asking: *does this height map, tested against a flat plate pulled straight up, undercut?* And the answer was structurally, unconditionally **no**. For the 49 reliefs I had. For the 49 million I didn't.

<PullQuote quote="A test whose answer is determined by the shape of the data rather than the content of the data is not a test. It's a constant with a stack trace." />

A hundred percent false-pass rate. Green the whole way down.

## The wrap changes everything

The relief doesn't stay flat. It gets wrapped around an 80 mm mug, and the mould around *that* splits into two halves at a vertical seam. Each half draws along its own horizontal axis, perpendicular to the seam.

That's the whole difference. On the flat plate, the pull direction is the same everywhere and always perpendicular to the base. On the cylinder, the base rotates underneath the pull. Stand at the front of the mug and the wall faces the puller squarely — you have the full slope budget. Walk around toward the seam and the wall turns edge-on, until it's nearly parallel to the direction you're pulling. A slope that had metres of margin at the front has none at the seam.

So the safe slope is a *function of position around the vessel*. Same artwork, same pipeline, different verdict depending on where it lands.

I re-ran the 49 against the wrapped geometry. All 49 locked.

From a hundred percent pass to a hundred percent fail, and neither number told me anything about the artwork. Both told me about my test.

## Two things fell out of that

**The first was placement, which is free.** Because the verdict depends on where the relief sits relative to the seam, sliding the artwork around the circumference changes the answer without touching a pixel. Centred inside a mould half, the median locked area across the set was 0.54%. Straddling the seam, 3.30%. A median factor of **6.2×** in locked area, bought by rotating the design.

That is a rounding error in compute and the largest single lever in the system. It's now a first-class step: work out the best placement, then judge.

**The second was a fix that costs almost nothing.** I added a node that clamps horizontal slope to the local draft budget — the budget being the position-dependent thing from the previous section, not a constant. Across the same 49 reliefs it took the count that lock from 49 to **zero**, moved the worst draft angle from −40° to +1.33°, and kept **95% of the fine detail**.

The alternative I'd been using — a general smoothing pass — kept 33% of fine detail for a sixth of the improvement. Which is what happens when you reach for a blunt instrument because the sharp one requires understanding the constraint.

## Three ways I got the fix wrong first

Worth writing down, because each one runs clean and lies to you.

**It has to run last.** There's a normalise step at the end of the pipeline that stretches the height range back to full scale. It scales every slope by exactly the factor the limiter just removed. Put the limiter before it and the pipeline runs, reports success, and emits a relief that locks precisely as much as before. Nothing errors. Nothing warns. The number in the log is correct and the object is broken.

**Only horizontal slope can lock a horizontally-drawn part.** Vertical slope still erodes the margin — it's in the normalisation — but it can never flip the sign of the draft. I spent time budgeting for both symmetrically before working out that one of them is a tax and the other is a cliff.

**Reconstruct in two dimensions, not row by row.** My first integrator satisfied the clamp exactly, row at a time. Every row was correct. The surface was covered in fine horizontal ridges that *no slope metric detected* — each row was individually within budget, and the metric never looked between rows. I only saw it when I rendered the thing as a solid under raking light and it looked like corduroy. It now solves a Poisson problem over both axes, alternating clamp and solve for twenty-four passes.

<PullQuote quote="Every row passed. The surface was ribbed. The metric had no opinion, because I'd only taught it to look one way." />

That's the same failure as the original bug, one level down. A measurement that can only see along one axis will report perfection on a defect that lives across it.

## What I'd take from this

The reflex when a check passes everything is to feel good and move on. The better reflex is suspicion: **ask what input would make it fail**, and if you can't construct one, you don't have a test.

I'd add a second, less comfortable one. My screen wasn't wrong because I misunderstood plaster. It was wrong because I tested the artwork in the coordinate frame where it was convenient to test — flat, axis-aligned, one pull direction — rather than the frame where it actually has to survive. The data was flat, so the test was flat. The object is a cylinder.

Forty-nine out of forty-nine is a beautiful number. It just wasn't measuring anything.
