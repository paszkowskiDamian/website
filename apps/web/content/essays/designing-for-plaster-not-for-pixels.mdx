Most 3D tools end at a picture. You model the thing, you light it, you render it, and the render is the deliverable — a beautiful lie that has never met a&nbsp;material.

The tool I've been building ends somewhere less flattering. Its last export is a set of thin printed shells that a person soaps, clamps, and fills with pottery plaster. What comes out of those shells is the working mould. What comes out of the *mould* is the cup. The render is three steps upstream of anything that matters, and it has no vote.

That single decision — the deliverable is tooling, not imagery — reorganises everything above it.

## The shape of the thing

Depth Map Studio is a grayscale relief editor bolted to a ceramic form modeller, sitting on top of a local generative pipeline. You author a relief as a height map: black is the floor, white is the peak, every grey in between is a millimetre of clay. You establish a vessel — a profile curve, revolved. You unwrap the vessel's surface flat, edit the whole surface at once as one continuous relief, and wrap it back.

![A generated bronze relief, its grayscale height map, and the height map rendered as a solid](/relief-picture-height-solid.jpg "Fig. 01 — The same relief as picture, height map, and solid. Only the third one tells you whether it can be made.")

Then the app has to answer the only question that isn't aesthetic: **can this be made?**

Not "does this render nicely." Not "is the mesh watertight." Can plaster be drawn off this surface without tearing it, and can the printed case be drawn off the plaster.

## Skipping a material

The traditional route from a master to a production mould goes through rubber. You make a master, you pour silicone over it to take a flexible case, you use the case to cast plaster working moulds, and you keep the case in a drawer because remaking it is a day's work.

We skip the rubber entirely. The 3D print **is** the case. Plaster goes straight into the printed shells.

<PullQuote quote="A silicone case exists because a plaster block is a one-off that has to be preserved. A file is not a one-off." />

That's the whole argument. The case in the drawer is an insurance policy against losing the master, and the insurance is unnecessary once the master is a parametric definition you can re-emit on demand. If a shell wears, cracks, or warps, you reprint it. The trade-off is real — printed shells are less forgiving about undercuts than rubber, which will stretch and let a bad geometry escape — but that's a constraint I'd rather have visible in the software than hidden in a material's tolerance for my mistakes.

Removing silicone removes a material, a cure time, a cost, and a step where the design quietly stops being a file.

## Where it gets hard

Here's the part I didn't see coming.

A relief that is perfectly safe to cast when it's lying flat can be impossible to cast the moment you wrap it around a mug.

A height map — a surface defined as one height per point on a plane — literally cannot undercut a straight pull along its own vertical axis. It's a mathematical impossibility. So if you test a flat relief for undercuts against a flat plate, every relief ever authored passes. Every single one.

Wrap that same relief onto a cylinder and split the mould into two halves, and each half now draws along its own horizontal axis. Travel around the circumference and the wall rotates away from that direction. At the front of the mug, a slope of thirty degrees is nothing. Near the seam, the wall is nearly *parallel* to the pull, and the same thirty degrees locks the mould shut.

Same artwork. Same pipeline. Different verdict depending on where it lands.

![Diagram comparing a flat pull direction with the rotating pull directions of a two-part mould on a cylinder](/pull-direction-flat-vs-wrapped.jpg "Fig. 02 — Flat, every slope is safe by construction. Wrapped, the budget runs out at the seam.")

Which means the safe slope isn't a constant you can print in the docs. It's a function of position around the vessel. And it means the flat check that passes everything isn't a lenient test — it's not a test at all. (I wrote about what that cost us [in more detail elsewhere](/essays/the-test-that-could-not-fail/).)

## Authoring against the constraint

The consequence for the UI is the interesting bit.

Because the relief is authored on the *unwrapped* surface, that unwrapped canvas can carry the constraint directly. Draw the seam lines on it. Shade it by how much slope each region can take before it locks. Now the draft budget is a thing you see while you're working, not a verdict delivered at the end by a validator that says *no* and offers nothing.

There's a nice inversion in this. The mould is usually treated as downstream — you design the object, then somebody works out how to make it. Here the mould's geometry reaches back up the pipeline and becomes a live overlay on the drawing surface. Part count and seam placement stop being settings the user picks and become *outputs* of the undercut analysis. The handle, for instance, is essentially always an undercut for the body halves, so it falls out of the analysis as its own insert piece rather than being declared as one.

<PullQuote quote="The constraint should be visible in the tool where you draw, not announced by a validator after you've finished." />

## Why this project

I keep coming back to the same thing: software that produces images is graded by taste, and software that produces objects is graded by physics. The second kind is harder and much more honest. A render can be wrong in a way that looks right forever. A mould is either drawable or it isn't, and the plaster will tell you within a day.

There's also something I find genuinely appealing about the loop being short enough to close in a home workshop. Author a relief in the browser. Export shells. Print overnight. Pour plaster. Cast a cup. Hold it. Notice that the finest ornament has come out as mush, and go back and fix the height map. That's a feedback loop with a real material in it, and there aren't many of those left in the kind of work I do.

The pictures were never the point. They were just the fastest thing to make.
